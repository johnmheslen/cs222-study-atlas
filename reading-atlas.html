How to read a pair. First identify the shared course question; next compare the authors’ units of analysis and evidence; finally ask what claim would be unwarranted if you applied their approach to a new setting. The title links lead to the official course schedule, which provides the public source links.

Foundations: purpose and level of analysis

Lecture 2 · Rittel & Webber (1973) + Schelling, Micromotives and Macrobehavior, Ch. 1

Rittel & Webber, “Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning.” The canonical account of wicked problems: problem definitions, evaluations, and interventions are entangled with social values. It prevents the course from treating simulation as a neutral optimizer.

Schelling, Micromotives and Macrobehavior. Individual choices can generate collective patterns that no individual intended. This establishes the micro-to-macro logic behind agent-based modeling and the importance of feedback.

Read for: how an explicit local rule can be informative without being a complete moral or causal account of a social problem.

Lecture 3 · Park et al. (2022) + Argyle et al. (2023)

“Social Simulacra.” Proposes populated prototypes for social-computing design: generate a breadth of possible social interactions before a system is launched. Its practical contribution is treating simulation as design exploration, not just prediction.

“Out of one, many.” Tests whether language models can simulate survey samples. It foregrounds the distinction between matching aggregate patterns and representing people faithfully.

Compare: a designer’s need for plausible interaction diversity versus a social scientist’s need for validated population-level inference.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 4 · Newell (1992) + Lehman et al. (2006)

Newell, “Précis of Unified Theories of Cognition.” Argues for integrated cognitive theories rather than isolated mechanisms. Read it as a standard of explanatory ambition: what must connect for an account to deserve the name “architecture”?

“A Gentle Introduction to Soar.” Makes that ambition concrete through symbolic state, problem solving, operators, learning, and impasses. It provides a contrast case for LLM-centered agent systems.

Compare: explicit, inspectable symbolic control with the flexible but opaque knowledge and generation of a language model.

Building agents and worlds

Lecture 5 · Park et al. (2023) + Sumers et al. (2024)

“Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior.” The course’s architectural anchor. It introduces a memory stream, retrieval based on recency/importance/relevance, reflection, and hierarchical planning to make language-model agents persist and coordinate in Smallville.

“Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents.” Places language agents in a wider design space of modules, control, memory, tools, and evaluation. It helps separate an implementation pattern from a claim about human cognition.

Read for: what each component buys, what information it assumes, and how one could ablate or test it.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 6 · Chang et al. (2024) + Louie et al. (2024)

“LLMs generate structurally realistic social networks but overestimate political homophily.” Shows that a generative model can reproduce some network structure while failing on a substantively important social mechanism. It is a useful warning against one-number validation.

“Roleplay-doh.” Lets domain experts author simulated patients through elicited principles. It centers a design question: how does knowledge of the setting enter an agent and its interaction environment?

Compare: an environment represented as a network with one represented as an expert-specified conversational situation.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 7 · Bates (1994) + Ashokkumar et al. (2024)

Bates, “The Role of Emotion in Believable Agents.” Defines believability as an interactive illusion of life, emphasizing reactivity, goals, emotion, and situated social competence rather than idealized intelligence.

“Predicting Results of Social Science Experiments Using Large Language Models.” Investigates whether LLMs can forecast experimental results. It moves the evaluative target toward empirical accuracy and makes data leakage and target selection central concerns.

Compare: a human judgment of character quality with a quantitatively testable prediction of an outcome.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 8 · Ansolabehere, Rodden & Snyder (2008) + optional GroupLens (1994)

“The Strength of Issues.” Studies preference stability, ideological constraint, and issue voting using multiple measures. It cautions against treating a person’s expressed attitude as a fixed essence.

GroupLens (optional). A foundational collaborative-filtering system. It offers a different kind of individual model: infer preferences from interaction traces and neighbors rather than narrating a complete persona.

Read for: competing ways of operationalizing a person—stated attitudes, stable dispositions, behavior, or relational signal.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 9 · Bruch & Atwell (2015) + Epstein & Axtell (1996), Chs. 1–2

“Agent-Based Models in Empirical Social Research.” Surveys how ABMs connect formal mechanisms to empirical social questions and why calibration, validation, and transparent assumptions matter.

Growing Artificial Societies. A foundational statement of generative social science: explain a macro pattern by demonstrating how it can emerge from agents and rules. “Grow it” is stronger than merely fitting a curve, but still requires empirical discipline.

Compare: explanation by generative mechanism versus resemblance to an observed aggregate.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 10 · Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (2001) + Kinder (2024)

“Initial sequencing and analysis of the human genome.” A case of large-scale shared scientific infrastructure. In this course it is an analogy for the datasets, standards, resources, and coordinated evaluation a simulation field may need.

Kinder on the 2023 Hollywood writers’ strike. A policy and labor lens on generative AI: technology’s effects are shaped by contracts, power, ownership, and collective action, not capability alone.

Read for: the institutional conditions under which a technical platform becomes socially accountable.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 11 · Lorenz (1993) + Holt & Roth (2004)

Lorenz, The Essence of Chaos. A conceptual introduction to nonlinear dynamics and sensitive dependence on initial conditions. It motivates uncertainty and sensitivity analysis rather than point forecasts.

“The Nash Equilibrium: A Perspective.” Explains the strategic-stability concept and its role in experimental and economic reasoning. It gives the course a vocabulary for mutually responsive agents.

Compare: equilibrium as a strategic relation and chaos as a property of dynamic evolution; a simulation can exhibit either, both, or neither.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 12 · Bostock, Ogievetsky & Heer (2011) + Wilensky (2021)

“D3: Data-Driven Documents.” Presents a declarative, data-bound approach to graphics. It exemplifies how a language can expose transformations and make complex work composable.

NetLogo User Manual. Documents a mature accessible language for agent-based models. Its primitives make agents, space, and procedures visible and authorable.

Read for: interface choices that make a model legible to its creator and critic; then ask what equivalent abstractions generative simulations need.

Limits, futures, and guest perspectives

Lecture 13 · Wang, Morgenstern & Dickerson (2024) + Santurkar et al. (2023)

“Large language models cannot replace human participants because they cannot portray identity groups.” Argues that model outputs cannot stand in for the lived experiences of identity groups. Its importance is normative and methodological: sampling a model is not sampling a community.

Santurkar et al. (ICML 2023). The assigned work examines whose perspectives are reflected in language-model outputs. It complements the first reading by treating representation as a question of distribution, provenance, and power.

Read for: the difference between generating language associated with a group and representing that group’s views or interests.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Lecture 14 · Schelling (2005) + Weiser (1991)

“An Astonishing Sixty Years: The Legacy of Hiroshima.” Schelling’s Nobel lecture examines the long, contingent legacy of nuclear weapons and deterrence. It encourages thinking in decades, institutions, and consequences under uncertainty.

“The Computer for the 21st Century.” Weiser’s ubiquitous-computing vision imagines computation integrated into everyday life. Here it frames AI simulation as potentially ambient social infrastructure rather than a confined research tool.

Read for: how a technology’s capabilities, social norms, and governance coevolve over time.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Guest lecture 1 · Morris & Brubaker (2024) + Manzini et al. (2024)

“Generative Ghosts.” Anticipates benefits and risks of AI representations of deceased people. It makes consent, grief, identity, and ongoing relational effects central design concerns.

“The Code That Binds Us.” Studies the appropriateness of human–AI assistant relationships. It provides concepts for evaluating when intimacy, dependence, or anthropomorphism may be supportive versus harmful.

Read for: why evaluation must include relationship-level effects, not only task success or behavioral plausibility.

Official schedule and public PDFs

Guest lecture 2 · Chang et al. (2021) + Chang et al. (2024)

“Mobility network models of COVID-19 explain inequities and inform reopening.” Uses fine-grained mobility networks to model epidemic outcomes and unequal exposure. It is an example of a grounded, policy-relevant network simulation with a clear empirical target.

“Learning production functions for supply chains with graph neural networks.” Applies graph-based learning to supply-chain structure and outcomes. It broadens the course’s notion of a simulation environment from spatial worlds to production networks.

Compare: mechanistic, empirically calibrated mobility modeling with learned graph representations; what is explanatory in each?

A reusable reading protocol

Before

  • Write the paper’s target: design exploration, explanation, prediction, or interaction?
  • State its unit of analysis and environment.
  • Identify what counts as evidence.

After

  • Write the strongest supported claim in one sentence.
  • Name one untested assumption and one ethical risk.
  • Design one counterexample or ablation that could change your confidence.
