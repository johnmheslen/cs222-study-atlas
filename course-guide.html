A Tour of Simulations: Past, Present, and Future

A simulation specifies an environment and the behaviors of entities within it, then lets their interaction unfold over time. Its distinctive value is controlled counterfactual exploration: change an initial condition or rule, run again, and compare possible worlds.

Core model

Think of world state as W(t) = environment state + each agent’s state. A transition rule advances the world: environment rules constrain agents; agents act and update the environment. This simple decomposition is a diagnostic tool for every later lecture.

  • Cellular automata: local rules create global patterns.
  • Game theory: strategically interdependent choices.
  • Agent-based models (ABMs): heterogeneous entities and interaction.
  • Generative agents: language models add flexible, natural-language-conditioned behavior.

What changed with LLMs?

LLMs can condition behavior on rich descriptions and generate open-ended language. They expand what can be modeled, but do not erase the need for a well-specified world, incentives, measurement, or validation. “Surprising” output is not automatically insight.

Review: Name one state variable, one environment rule, and one agent behavior for a campus-food simulation. Which of those would an LLM actually determine?

Wicked Problems

Simulation is motivated here by problems whose objectives, stakeholders, and effects cannot be cleanly separated. Social-media governance and pandemic policy illustrate how an intervention can create a new problem through feedback and competing values.

Rittel & Webber’s warning

Wicked problems lack a single definitive formulation or stopping rule; alternatives are judged better or worse, not true or false; and interventions are consequential rather than cheap experiments. Each case is historically and socially particular.

This does not mean “anything goes.” It means a model must declare whose outcomes count, which mechanisms are in scope, and what uncertainty remains.

Simulation’s role

It can make a causal story inspectable, surface unintended consequences, and support deliberation over scenarios. It cannot select society’s values or guarantee that a stylized model transfers to the real world.

Review: For content moderation, draw one feedback loop connecting ranking, engagement, exposure, trust, and policy response. Where would a model most likely be incomplete?

Individuals, Groups, and Populations

“Agent” is not a fixed natural unit. A simulation can model a person, a household, an organization, a demographic segment, or a population distribution. The choice changes the claims the model can support.

Three levels of analysis

  • Individual: biography, goals, memory, and situated choice. Rich but expensive and hard to validate.
  • Group: roles, norms, coordination, and relationships. The key challenge is emergent interaction.
  • Population: distributions and aggregate outcomes. Scalable, but can hide heterogeneity and mechanism.

Granularity should be driven by the research question, not by a reflex to maximize detail.

Reading connection

Social Simulacra uses model-generated personas and interactions to prototype a social system before real users are recruited at scale. Argyle et al. ask whether a language model can simulate survey samples. Together they sharpen the tradeoff: useful aggregate resemblance is different from representing a particular person.

Review: When could a population model give the right average while making the wrong policy recommendation?

Cognitive Architectures

Before generative AI, cognitive architectures tried to make an integrated account of mind: perception, memory, goals, reasoning, learning, and action. Their lesson is architectural: an agent needs an organized control loop, not just isolated capability demos.

Newell and Soar

Newell’s “unified theories” ambition is to explain a broad range of cognition with a shared set of mechanisms. Soar operationalizes this with problem spaces, operators, working memory, long-term knowledge, and learning from impasses. Such systems privilege explicit representations and traceable reasoning.

Bridge to modern agents

LLMs supply flexible knowledge and language, but a prompt alone offers weak persistence, control, and auditability. Modern agent architectures reintroduce memory stores, retrieval, planning, tools, and feedback loops—often without claiming that they are cognitively faithful.

Review: Contrast a “model of human cognition” with an “engineering architecture inspired by cognition.” Why does the distinction matter for scientific claims?

Lecture deck · source PDF

Architecting Generative Agents

The course turns the LLM into one component of a longer-lived agent. The generative-agent pattern is a loop: observe the world, record experiences, retrieve relevant memories, reflect, plan, act, and update the world.

Memory is a design problem

A memory stream stores observations as records rather than cramming everything into a prompt. Retrieval ranks memories using a combination of recency, importance, and semantic relevance. Reflection compresses recurring observations into higher-level conclusions; planning decomposes broad intentions into progressively finer actions.

The point is bounded context: deciding what to bring forward changes behavior.

Failure modes to track

  • Inaccurate or invented memories become self-reinforcing.
  • Importance scoring encodes an implicit theory of what matters.
  • Plans can be fluent yet infeasible in the world.
  • Prompt and model changes can alter behavior unexpectedly.

Review: Propose a memory-retrieval test that distinguishes relevance from mere lexical similarity.

Interactive Worlds

An environment is the set of settings and choices an agent can perceive. It is not neutral scenery: its affordances, constraints, presentation order, costs, and social context all shape observed behavior.

Environment determines opportunity

Choices carry opportunity costs in money, time, attention, emotional energy, and social capital. A model that gives agents endless budget or feeds them posts one at a time can make them appear unrealistically eager to buy, like, or engage. That is a world-model error as much as an agent-model error.

Representations

Traditional systems used grids or abstract games. Generative systems use surveys, experimental vignettes, conversations, social feeds, or spatial worlds such as Smallville’s scene graph. Desired environments are both richly grounded and scalable—an unresolved tension.

Review: In a simulated hiring market, list three missing constraints that would make “agent bias” impossible to interpret.

Believability vs. Accuracy

These are separate targets. A believable agent produces an “illusion of life”—reactive, emotional, goal-directed, socially situated behavior. An accurate simulation reproduces or predicts a relevant empirical outcome.

Why not collapse them?

A delightful companion can be persuasive without forecasting real people. Conversely, a population model may predict an aggregate statistic while feeling mechanical. Evaluation must follow the intended use: qualitative judgments and experience studies for believability; preregistered holdouts, replication targets, and calibrated uncertainty for accuracy.

Validation cautions

Open-ended individual behavior has many possible axes; group dynamics are especially difficult; and benchmark studies may have entered model training data. A positive result should specify what is matched, against which baseline, and under what distribution shift.

Review: Give an example of a simulation that is believable but dangerous to treat as accurate.

Lecture deck · source PDF

Models of Individuals

To model a person is to choose a representation: traits, demographics, attitudes, biography, observed choices, social ties, or a mixture. Representation determines what behavior the system can plausibly generate and which privacy or stereotyping hazards it creates.

Stability and context

The Ansolabehere–Rodden–Snyder reading complicates any simple “preference profile”: attitudes vary in stability and may not line up neatly into an ideology. Individual models should distinguish durable dispositions from context-sensitive responses, and should make uncertainty visible.

Design questions

  • What data or theory licenses each attribute?
  • Will the system infer protected or intimate attributes?
  • Is the model intended to stand for a person, a type, or a task-specific response distribution?
  • Can the represented subject contest, inspect, or revoke the model?

Review: Why can a detailed persona still be a weak predictive model?

Lecture deck · source PDF

AgentBank-CS222

This module makes the representation problem concrete: create a shared collection of agents that can stand in for a community. It reframes the task from building a single character to governing a reusable social resource.

ABM lineage

Empirical ABMs seek explanatory mechanisms: specify agents, interaction rules, and institutions, then observe macro patterns. Epstein and Axtell’s “generative social science” asks whether a social pattern can be grown from explicit micro rules; Bruch and Atwell survey the methodological tension between realism and tractability.

Bank governance

An agent bank needs provenance, consent boundaries, intended-use restrictions, versioning, access controls, and evaluation. It should not quietly turn people into immutable profiles. A bank can support counterfactual analysis only if its construction and sampling assumptions are transparent.

Review: Write a minimal “agent card” with purpose, source, limitations, and prohibited uses.

Generative Agent-Based Models

Generative ABMs combine flexible individual models with ABM’s focus on interaction and emergence. Their promise is a richer behavioral substrate; their risk is confusing plausible language with a calibrated causal mechanism.

Platform analogy

The lecture uses large shared scientific infrastructures to ask what it would take for agent simulation to mature: common data representations, interoperable components, reproducible protocols, documentation, and a community that can contest claims. The question is institutional as well as technical.

Worker and social consequences

Simulating creative or professional activity raises labor, ownership, bargaining, and displacement issues. The Hollywood-writers reading provides a concrete lens: workers may negotiate not only about outputs but about training data, digital replicas, and control of future uses.

Review: Which model components need to be shared for another group to reproduce—not merely rerun—your result?

Equilibria and Butterflies

Once agents interact, behavior cannot be read directly from one prompt. Strategic incentives can settle into equilibria; nonlinear feedback can amplify tiny differences. Both features demand many runs, sensitivity analysis, and humility about long-horizon forecasts.

Two lenses

Nash equilibrium is a state in which no actor benefits from unilateral deviation, useful for reasoning about strategic stability. Chaos highlights sensitive dependence on initial conditions, useful for reasoning about forecasting limits. Neither says a social system is simple or permanently stable.

Practical implication

Report distributions, not a single cinematic run. Vary seeds, prompts, population composition, network structure, and uncertain parameters. Identify qualitative patterns that survive rather than treating one narrative trace as evidence.

Review: What result would count as robust if 30 runs reach different stories but the same distributional outcome?

Language and Schema of Simulations

Complex simulations need a language that lets people define agents, worlds, interactions, data, and outputs without obscuring the model. A schema is both an authoring aid and a contract about what exists in the simulated world.

Inspirations

D3 separates data from visual encoding and makes transformations explicit. NetLogo supplies agent, patch, and procedure primitives that make ABMs approachable and inspectable. The course asks what an analogous vocabulary for generative simulation should expose: personas, memory, norms, networks, affordances, objectives, interventions, and measures.

Good schemas support critique

If “culture” or “identity” is tucked into an opaque prompt, reviewers cannot inspect how it operates. Explicit structure supports reuse, comparison, debugging, and ethical review—but a rigid schema can also erase what it cannot express.

Review: Draft five fields for an intervention schema. Which fields must be machine-readable, and which need human explanation?

Ethics and Limitations

The course rejects the idea that a model’s fluency makes it an ethical substitute for people. Simulation can amplify representation harms, enable surveillance or manipulation, leak private information, and lend false authority to decisions.

Identity is not a prompt variable

Wang, Morgenstern, and Dickerson argue that LLMs cannot replace human participants when identity-group experience is the relevant object of study. Group membership is lived, contextual, and political—not a bundle of generic textual associations.

Ethical review checklist

  • Who is represented, excluded, or made legible?
  • What consent, provenance, and privacy protections apply?
  • Could outputs affect a represented group or individual?
  • What evidence limits the claim, and who can challenge it?
  • Is human participation necessary and non-substitutable?

Review: Identify a decision for which an AI simulation might assist deliberation but must never replace stakeholder participation.

Lecture deck · source PDF

Simulating Ourselves and Our Societies With AI

The closing lecture asks whether behavioral simulation could become a major AI application: a medium for exploring institutional choices, rehearsing interactions, designing systems, and imagining futures. The opportunity is inseparable from governance.

Two future-facing frames

Schelling’s Hiroshima lecture emphasizes the long arc of consequences, deterrence, and choices under grave uncertainty. Weiser’s ubiquitous-computing vision imagines computing receding into everyday life. Together they frame agent simulation as infrastructure that can become ambient before society has settled its norms.

Course synthesis

A credible simulation requires: a question worth asking; a declared unit of analysis; agents and environments matched to that question; interaction rules; measurement and validation; uncertainty analysis; and ethical governance. LLMs alter the implementation toolkit, not those obligations.

Final review: Make a one-page proposal for a simulation you would trust enough to inform a decision. What is its strongest claim—and its strictest no-claim boundary?
